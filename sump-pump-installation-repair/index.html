Why is there water in my basement after it rains?

Groundwater rises when the soil around a foundation is saturated, and it looks for the lowest path. That path is often the joint where the wall meets the floor, a drain inlet, or a floor crack. A sump pump sits in a pit at the low point, collects that water, and pushes it out through a pipe before it spreads.

If the pit is dry and water comes in anyway, the pit is in the wrong place or the home has no pump. If the pit fills and the pump does not start, the pump, float switch or power is at fault. If the pump runs constantly, it may be undersized or the check valve is failing.

A basement leak is not always groundwater. Roof gutters, a downspout dumping beside the house, or a leaking pipe can look the same. Our leak detection page explains how we separate a plumbing leak from rain and soil water.

Urgent situation

When a sump pump failure is an emergency

Call right away if the pump will not start while the pit is filling, if water is already on the floor, or if the pump runs and the pit keeps rising. Check the cord and the breaker or GFCI outlet first, and make sure the discharge pipe outside is not frozen or blocked.

Do not walk through standing water near electrical equipment, and do not try to lift a pump with the power on. Move boxes, rugs and electronics to higher ground, and photograph the water level and damage for your insurer. Your policy decides what is covered, and sump failures are sometimes treated differently from other water losses.

Installing a new sump pump or pit

A new installation means cutting into the slab, setting a basin, adding a pump with a float switch and check valve, and running a discharge line outside. Where the water goes matters. It should discharge away from the foundation and not toward a neighbor, and never into the sanitary sewer.

Sizing depends on how fast water enters the pit. Cast-iron pumps tend to run cooler and longer, while thermoplastic ones cost less. A submersible pump sits in the pit and runs quieter than a pedestal pump, which sits on top.

Permit rules vary, so ask the building department before cutting the slab or changing the discharge. St. Louis County Plumbing Code, Chapter 1103, as adopted by the City of Wildwood, governs the plumbing portion.

Battery backup and storm power loss

A pump needs electricity, and storms cut electricity. A backup is the answer.

  • Battery backup pump.

    A second pump runs on a deep-cycle battery when the power goes out. It has a limited run time, so it is a bridge, not a generator.

  • Water-powered backup.

    Some systems use municipal water pressure to run a pump. They need adequate pressure, and homes on private wells are not suited to them.

  • Alarm.

    A high-water alarm tells you before the pit overflows. It is inexpensive and worth adding.

  • Battery care.

    Batteries wear out in a few years. Test and replace on schedule, or the backup fails when you need it.

Sewage ejectors and basement bathrooms

A sewage ejector is a different pump. It lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the main drain, and it needs a sealed, vented pit. Do not mix it up with a sump pump, which handles groundwater.

What affects the price of sump pump work

A float swap and a full basin installation are very different jobs. These factors drive the price.

Repair or replacement.

A float switch costs less than a new pump, and a new basin costs more.

Pump type and horsepower.

Larger or cast-iron pumps cost more than basic ones.

Slab and discharge work.

Breaking concrete and running a long discharge line add labor.

Backup system.

A battery or water-powered backup adds equipment.

Time of the call.

Storm-night calls cost more than scheduled visits.
