Tub faucet guide

Bathtub Faucet Leak Repair in Wildwood, MO

A dripping tub faucet is usually a worn cartridge, washer, or seat, and many homeowners can replace it in under an hour with the water shut off. The fix depends on your valve type: a single-handle valve uses a cartridge, an older two-handle faucet uses rubber washers and a brass seat, and a diverter that leaks from the spout needs its own repair.

Water that shows on the ceiling below, soaks the wall behind the tub, or keeps flowing after the handle is closed fully is different. That points to a failed valve body or a leaking supply line inside the wall, and it is time to call.

Start with the type of valve you have

Look at the handles. Two handles, one hot and one cold, usually mean a compression or ceramic disc valve. One handle that pushes in and out and turns for temperature is a cartridge or ball valve. A knob that pulls up for the shower is the diverter, and it can leak into the tub spout even if the main valve is fine.

Remove the handle cap and take a photo of the parts before you touch anything. The brand name on the trim, often stamped on the escutcheon or inside the cartridge, helps you buy the right replacement. Take the old cartridge to the store to match it.

Swapping a leaking cartridge

  1. 1

    Shut off the water.

    Close the bathroom shutoff or the main, then open the faucet to drain the line.

  2. 2

    Pull the handle and trim.

    Take off the cap, remove the screw, slide off the handle, and unscrew the trim plate.

  3. 3

    Remove the cartridge.

    Pull out the retaining clip or nut, then lift or twist the cartridge out. Some need a puller tool.

  4. 4

    Install the match and reassemble.

    Seat the new cartridge in the same orientation, secure it, replace the trim, and turn the water on slowly.

What to do if you cannot shut the water off

A tub faucet whose stops do not work turns a small job into a bigger one.

  • Close the main water valve.

    It is usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house. Know where yours is.

  • Check for fixture stops.

    Some homes have small shutoff screws behind an access panel in the next room. Not all do.

  • Do not force a stuck handle.

    Cranking it can break the stem inside the wall.

  • Place a bucket and towel.

    A slow flow is manageable, but stop if it grows.

  • Call a plumber.

    If the main valve will not close either, treat it as an emergency.

Signs the leak is inside the wall

A brown stain on the ceiling under the bathroom, bubbling paint, a musty smell, or a floor that feels soft beside the tub all point to hidden water. Dripping at the spout alone does not cause those signs. Stop using the tub, photograph the damage, and call, because wet framing and drywall get worse with every shower.

Why a quick fix sometimes fails twice

A new cartridge in a pitted valve body can still leak, because the housing is scored. Reusing a worn seat under a new washer is the same trap. If the same drip returns within weeks, the valve itself is the problem.

Over-tightening also causes trouble. Cranking a trim screw can crack a plastic cartridge, and a wrench on a brass nut can distort the seat. Snug is enough, and thread tape is only for tapered pipe threads, not the cartridge.
