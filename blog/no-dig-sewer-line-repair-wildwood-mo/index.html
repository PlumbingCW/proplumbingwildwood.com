No-Dig Sewer Line Repair in Wildwood, MO: When It Fits and When It Doesn't

No-dig sewer line repair fixes a buried drain pipe from the inside or by pulling a new pipe through the old path, so the yard stays mostly intact. The two common methods are a cured-in-place liner and pipe bursting. Both need a camera inspection first, because they only work on a line that is still mostly intact and connected.

If your camera footage shows a crushed section, a deep belly that holds water, or a pipe that has separated, expect a short trench instead. The rest of this guide shows how to tell the difference before you agree to anything.

What the camera has to show before anyone quotes lining

A sewer camera is a small waterproof lens on a flexible rod. It goes in through a cleanout, and a good inspection records the whole run from the house to the connection point. You want to see the footage, not just hear a summary of it.

Ask the plumber to note the distance of each defect from the cleanout. That number tells you whether a repair is one short section or the whole line. It also tells the crew where to dig if a trench turns out to be the better answer.

A locator on the surface can mark the pipe path and depth. Depth matters because pipe bursting needs pits at both ends, and a very deep run changes the plan.

Lining versus bursting in plain terms

Cured-in-place lining

A resin-soaked tube is pulled into the cleaned pipe, inflated, and hardened so it forms a new pipe inside the old one. It reduces the inside diameter slightly, but a smooth wall usually flows better than a rough, root-chewed one. It suits pipe that is sound enough to act as a mold.

Pipe bursting

A cone-shaped head is pulled through the old line, cracks it outward, and drags a new plastic pipe in behind it. This can restore the full diameter and even upsize the pipe. It needs entry and exit pits, and it is a poor fit when other utilities cross the path closely.

What you can do before the plumber arrives

These steps cost nothing and protect your floors, your insurance claim, and the footage quality.

  • Stop sending water down the drains.

    Hold off on laundry, dishwashing, and long showers. Every gallon has to go somewhere, and a blocked line sends it to the lowest fixture.

  • Find the cleanout.

    It is a capped pipe, often near the foundation, in a basement floor, or in a crawlspace. Do not open it if sewage is already rising, because the cap can release a surge.

  • Photograph everything.

    Take pictures of standing water, damaged belongings, and the date. Your policy decides what is covered, and photos make the conversation easier.

  • Keep people and pets out of wet areas.

    Backup water carries bacteria. Wear gloves and boots if you must go in, and wash well afterward.

  • Note where the sewer gas smell is strongest.

    A smell near one drain can point to a dry trap or a vent problem. A smell everywhere points toward the main line. Tell the plumber which one you have.

How a lining job unfolds

  1. 1

    Clear the pipe.

    A cable or a hydro jet removes roots and scale so the liner can seat against bare pipe wall.

  2. 2

    Re-inspect with the camera.

    The crew confirms the run is open and rechecks for collapses that the first pass missed behind the roots.

  3. 3

    Install and cure the liner.

    The tube is positioned, inflated, and hardened with steam, hot water, or light, depending on the product.

  4. 4

    Reopen side connections and take final footage.

    Branch lines are cut open from inside, and a closing video shows the finished pipe from end to end.

What moves the price of a no-dig repair

We are not quoting dollar figures here, because the footage drives the number. Length of the damaged run is the first factor, then pipe diameter and depth. The number of bends, cleanouts, and side connections also changes the labor.

Surface restoration matters too. A repair that needs two small pits costs differently from one that crosses a driveway or a retaining wall. Ask for a written scope that names the method, the footage covered, and what is included for cleanup.

Permits are part of the picture. If your insurance or a city program contributes, ask what paperwork they require before work begins. Call our 24/7 line and we will walk through the footage and give a clear price before work starts.

Wildwood sewers: what to know locally

The City of Wildwood says sewer service is provided by MSD Project Clear, and that the city does not manage utility records, so ask MSD about the main on your street. The city's Master Plan says the soils in much of the city are unsuitable for individual septic systems and that some subdivisions use package treatment plants. If your home is not on a public sewer, lining a lateral will not apply, so confirm your arrangement first.

City code section 510.080 says buildings with plumbing must connect to a public sewer when one is available, and it counts a sanitary sewer line within 200 feet as available. That matters if you are on an older private system and a main runs nearby.

An April 2023 city memo studied whether Wildwood would join the St. Louis County Sewer Lateral Repair Program. It cited a county fee of $28 to $50 per year and an average county repair cost of $3,330. We could not confirm that Wildwood joined, so ask the city before assuming any program help.

Neighboring cities run their own rules. Ellisville states that the homeowner is responsible for a leaking lateral, and Ballwin reimburses repairs up to a set cap through an application. Neither rule is Wildwood's, but both show why to ask before you sign a contract. See our pages for Ellisville and Ballwin for those programs.

When to stop and call a licensed plumber

Call as soon as you see sewage in a floor drain, repeated slow drains across the house, or a gurgle in a toilet when another fixture drains. Those signs point to the main line, and a blocked main can flood a basement fast.
