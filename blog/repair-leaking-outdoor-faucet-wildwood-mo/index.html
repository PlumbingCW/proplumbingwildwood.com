Outdoor faucet guide

Repair a Leaking Outdoor Faucet in Wildwood, MO

Where the water appears tells you how to fix a leaking outdoor faucet. Water around the handle usually means a loose packing nut or a worn packing washer, and you can fix that yourself. A drip from the spout with the handle off points to a worn seat washer. Water inside the wall or a spray when the hose is on points to a cracked stem or pipe, and that is a call to a plumber.

Start by closing the shutoff valve inside the house for that faucet, if you have one. If you do not, close the main. Then use the table below to match the leak to the cause.

The packing nut fix, step by step

  1. 1

    Shut off the supply.

    Close the interior valve, then open the outdoor faucet to drain the line and relieve pressure.

  2. 2

    Snug the packing nut.

    Use a wrench to tighten the nut behind the handle a quarter turn at a time. Stop when the leak stops.

  3. 3

    Replace the packing if tightening fails.

    Remove the handle screw, handle, and nut, then wrap graphite packing string or replace the packing washer and reassemble.

  4. 4

    Test with the water slowly on.

    Turn the supply back on slowly, open the faucet, and check the stem and spout for drips before you leave.

Frost-free spigot basics

A frost-free sillcock puts the shutoff valve seat deep inside the warm part of the wall, on the end of a long stem. When you close the handle, the valve shuts inside the house, and the water in the outer barrel drains out. That only works if the faucet is mounted with a slight downward tilt and the hose is removed.

Leaving a hose connected traps water in the barrel, and it can freeze and split the tube. The split often stays hidden until spring, when you turn the faucet on and water sprays inside the wall instead of out of the spout. A hose-end shutoff or a splitter that holds the hose in place can cause the same problem.

Before you take anything apart

Shut off the water first, and keep your hands out of any wet area where an electrical outlet or an extension cord is nearby. Do not force a seized handle. If a screw spins, a stem is stuck, or the valve will not shut, stop and call, because forcing it can turn a drip into a pipe break.

A checklist for the first warm day of spring

A faucet that sat through winter should be tested before the hose goes back on.

  • Open the faucet slowly.

    Turn on the interior valve and watch the wall and the basement ceiling below for dampness.

  • Look at the stem.

    A spray from the packing nut area is a quick fix. A spray from the pipe behind the faucet is a stop and call.

  • Check the water meter.

    With every fixture off, a moving meter dial points to a hidden leak somewhere in the house.

  • Photograph damage.

    Take pictures of any wet wall or ceiling for your insurance conversation.

  • Note the age of the faucet.

    A very old unit with corroded threads is usually cheaper to replace than to chase.

Winter prep that prevents the spring leak

Before the first hard freeze, remove every hose, splitter, and nozzle. Close the interior shutoff valve to each outdoor faucet and open the outdoor handle to drain the line. If a faucet has no interior shutoff, ask a plumber about adding one.

Older standard spigots without frost-free stems are the highest risk. Replacing one with a frost-free model before winter is cheaper than repairing a burst behind the wall. Faucet covers help on cold nights, but they do not replace draining the line.
