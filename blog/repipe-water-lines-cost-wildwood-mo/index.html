Most Wildwood houses do not need a repipe

A repipe replaces most or all of the supply lines inside a house, the pipes that carry water to sinks, showers and appliances. It makes sense when the pipe itself is failing, not when a single fitting leaks or a faucet drips. One leak in a copper line or one failed connection is usually a spot repair.

The decision comes down to pipe material and pattern. A house with repeated leaks in different spots, discolored water or low pressure from corroded pipe is a candidate. A house with a single leak, original copper in good shape or newer PEX is not.

What affects the price to repipe water lines

Every repipe quote rests on these five things. Know them before you compare numbers.

Pipe material.

PEX is flexible and quick to run, copper is rigid and takes more labor and material, and the price gap between them depends on the home and market at the time.

House size and layout.

More bathrooms, fixtures and floors mean more pipe and more connections. A long run to a second-floor bath costs more than a short run to a slab-side kitchen.

Wall and ceiling access.

If pipes run through finished walls, drywall must be opened, then patched and painted. Open basements and crawlspaces keep that work low.

Shutoffs, fixtures and the water heater.

Old shutoff valves, fixture supply lines and sometimes the water heater connection are replaced at the same time, since you will not want to open walls twice.

Permit and inspection.

Replaced plumbing needs a permit through the city and county process, and a licensed plumber normally pulls it. Your price should name who handles it.

Sudden bursts and the water damage they cause are often covered under a homeowner policy, but a repipe to replace aging or corroded pipe usually is not, and gradual leaks often are excluded. Your policy decides what is covered, so ask your agent before work begins.

How to tell what pipe is in your house

You can learn a lot in ten minutes near the water heater, the meter and under the kitchen sink.

  • Copper.

    Reddish-brown metal, hard, with soldered joints. Green crust at a joint is corrosion.

  • Galvanized steel.

    Dull gray metal with threaded joints. A magnet sticks to it, and it often feels rough inside when cut open.

  • PEX.

    Flexible plastic in red, blue or white, with metal or plastic crimp rings at connections.

  • Polybutylene.

    Gray, bluish or black flexible plastic with a plastic or metal ring fitting. If you find it, ask a plumber to check it.

  • CPVC.

    Rigid cream or light yellow plastic, glued at joints, often on hot and cold lines.

  • Mixed pipe.

    Many houses have more than one material. A repair near an old section may need a transition fitting.

What happens on a repipe job

  1. 1

    Survey and plan.

    The plumber traces each line from the meter and heater to every fixture, decides where walls must be opened, and chooses the new routes.

  2. 2

    Shut off and drain.

    Water to the house is turned off and the lines are drained, so you will have no water during the main work.

  3. 3

    Run new lines.

    New pipe goes in through attics, basements and wall cavities, and old pipe is capped or removed where it is easy to reach.

  4. 4

    Pressure test and permit inspection.

    The lines are tested for leaks and the work is inspected as the permit requires.

  5. 5

    Patch and flush.

    Wall openings are patched, fixtures reconnected and the system flushed. Painting and finish repair are often separate.

Alternatives that cost less than a full repipe

If one wall holds the failing section, a partial repipe replaces just that run. Some homes can swap the main supply to a new pressure-regulated line and leave good branch pipes alone. Leak repair with a pressure test, plus a plan to watch for more, is the cheapest route when the damage is local.
