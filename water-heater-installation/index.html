Decisions to make before installation day

Most delays and extra charges come from choices made late. Settle these first.

  • Fuel.

    Natural gas, propane, standard electric and heat pump electric each need different supply and venting. Gas availability at a specific address varies, so we confirm it on the visit.

  • Location.

    Basement, garage, closet or attic. Each affects drain access, vent path and what happens when the tank eventually leaks.

  • Capacity.

    Size by household and peak use, not by what was there. A remodel that adds a soaking tub changes the math.

  • Heat pump needs.

    A hybrid unit wants open airflow, a condensate drain and a space the manufacturer lists as suitable.

  • Water source.

    City supply or a private well changes the sediment and scale you should plan for.

  • Timing.

    A scheduled install can be coordinated with a remodel, drywall or an inspection.

Venting, combustion air and carbon monoxide

A gas or propane water heater burns fuel inside your house, so the flue must carry exhaust outside without leaks. Atmospheric-draft units rely on warm gas rising through a vent that slopes upward all the way. Power-vented and direct-vent models use a fan and a sealed path, and they allow more flexibility in where the heater sits.

Combustion air matters too. A tank in a tight utility closet can starve for air and backdraft exhaust into the room. We test draft after installation. If anyone in the house smells gas, leave the building and call Spire at 800-887-4173 or 911 before calling a plumber.

Expansion tanks, pressure and the relief valve

Water expands when it heats. In an open plumbing system the extra volume pushes back into the supply main, but many homes have a check valve, backflow device or pressure reducing valve that closes that path. Without somewhere to go, pressure climbs every heating cycle and can stress the tank, the fittings and the relief valve.

A small expansion tank on the cold line absorbs the swing. We check static pressure at the house and set the tank's air charge to match. The temperature and pressure relief valve also needs a full-size discharge pipe that terminates where it cannot scald anyone.

Code and permits in Wildwood

The City of Wildwood has adopted the St. Louis County Plumbing Code (Chapter 1103, adopted 2019) as its plumbing code. The city's permit page lists new or replaced plumbing among work that needs a building permit, and it points to St. Louis County Public Works for details.

Permit rules vary, so ask the building department before a new installation. We install to code and can tell you what we see, but we do not decide the permit. Also verify that any plumber you hire is licensed. St. Louis County licenses plumbers through its Board of Plumbing Examiners in the Department of Transportation and Public Works.

New location or remodel: how plumbing overlaps

Moving a heater, adding a bathroom or finishing a basement involves new supply lines, drains and sometimes a gas branch. Those pieces overlap with our general plumbing installation work, so it helps to plan them together. Roughing in pipe before drywall is cheaper and tidier than cutting open a finished wall.

Wildwood's median home was built in 1992 according to the Census Bureau, and about 42.8 percent predate 1990. Homes from that period often have a heater in a basement or garage utility area. If you are relocating it, tell us now so we can check existing pipe sizes and shutoffs.

Hard water and well water at the new heater

Missouri American Water supplies the city. Its 2023 report lists total hardness from 66 to 150 ppm at Meramec plants and 82 to 226 ppm at Missouri River plants. Homes west of Route 109 and some east of it are on private wells, where hardness and iron must be tested. Ask your utility for its annual water quality report, or test your tap.

Why it matters at installation: scale builds on elements and tank floors. Adding a flush valve and shutoffs now makes later maintenance easier, and a softener or filter can be piped in at the same visit.
